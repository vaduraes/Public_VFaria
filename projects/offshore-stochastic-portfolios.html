AI scenario generation and risk-averse offshore portfolios

Artificial intelligence (AI) · Renewable energy · Uncertainty · Optimization · Published 2023

A wind, wave, and ocean-current portfolio can perform well on average yet produce very little during unfavorable conditions. Minimizing overall variability does not directly address that risk: it treats unusually high and unusually low generation as equally undesirable. This study focuses instead on the days when combined output is lowest. It links an AI model that generates plausible joint resource conditions off North Carolina to an optimization model that chooses where to place offshore devices.

Generating joint offshore scenarios

Wind, wave, and ocean-current records overlap from 2007 to 2013. We converted them into daily generation maps and trained a convolutional generative adversarial network (GAN) across more than 500 potential sites. A useful synthetic day must keep relationships between places and resources: a portfolio’s benefit depends on whether its sites tend to have low output together, not just on each site’s average production.

The generated maps were checked against historical site averages, relationships between pairs of sites and resources, and a distribution-similarity measure. In the paper’s tests, the GAN retained much of the observed joint variation and matched the historical data more closely than the tested multivariate normal generator. The maps are independent daily samples, not a simulated sequence of future years.

Choosing sites for low-generation days

The portfolio model asks: if a given mix of turbines must be built, where should they go to improve output in the hardest conditions? For each tested mix, it chooses the number of wind, wave, and ocean-current devices at each eligible site. The total number of devices of each technology is fixed for that comparison. Site limits, collection-system reach, and an upper bound on levelized cost of energy (LCOE) constrain the choices.

The objective uses conditional value at risk (CVaR) of generation. For example, with 10,000 sampled days, it averages the portfolio’s output on the 500 lowest-generation days and tries to make that average as high as possible. Repeating the optimization under different cost ceilings shows the tradeoff between LCOE and performance on those days.

Findings

  • Variance is not a substitute for low-output risk. At similar cost levels in the tested portfolio, the variance-minimizing choices generated less electricity on the weakest days than choices optimized for the lowest 5% directly.

  • The number of scenarios matters. The paper compared samples containing roughly 10 and 30 years’ worth of generated daily maps. With the larger sample, the estimated cost-risk frontier varied less across 500 fresh resamples. These are sample sizes, not continuous synthetic weather histories.

  • Resource diversity improved the tested portfolios. Combinations of wind, wave, and ocean current achieved better low-generation scores than the less diverse mixes in the paper’s comparison, although wave and ocean-current options also had higher assumed costs.
