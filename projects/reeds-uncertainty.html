Uncertainty in electricity investment decisions

Energy planning · Optimization · Uncertainty · Conference paper: 2026

Research period: August 2024–August 2025, NREL. Collaborators and supervisors: Brian Sergi and Patrick R. Brown. Supported by the U.S. Department of Energy Grid Deployment Office.

Electricity investments last for decades, but the assumptions used to plan them can change much sooner. This project asks which features of a future U.S. electricity system persist across uncertain inputs, and which depend on a particular combination of demand growth, technology costs, fuel prices, renewable siting, and federal policy.

From uncertain inputs to investment outcomes

I developed Monte Carlo workflows in Python and GAMS integrated with the ReEDS capacity expansion model. The conference study evaluates 1,137 completed simulations, co-optimizing generation, storage, and transmission from 2025 through 2050. It represents the contiguous United States with 132 model zones, representative operating days, and additional periods of system stress.

Each simulation samples a complete trajectory between low, middle, and high input projections, using the same weights across years to maintain consistency over time. The analysis compares flat and triangular distributions, and tests whether state-level demand and siting assumptions move together or independently. Separate policy scenarios reuse the same random draws, allowing policy effects to be compared while holding other sampled assumptions fixed. The policy baseline reflects August 2025 assumptions.

What drives the range of outcomes

  • Capacity additions vary widely. Solar PV, land-based wind, and gas dominate average capacity additions in the current-policy cases. For gas and wind in one central scenario, the 5th-to-95th-percentile span of 2050 capacity exceeds a factor of two.

  • Correlation changes the range of national outcomes. When states move together, their demand and siting changes reinforce one another. Independent state draws partly offset each other, narrowing aggregate variation. Distribution shape and regional correlation affect the spread more than the central values.

  • Policy changes can exceed the effects of the sampling choices. Continuing the tax credits represented in the study increases median 2050 PV and wind capacity by roughly 200 GW each relative to its matched current-policy case, and approximately doubles median storage capacity.

  • Inputs interact. Low nuclear construction costs alone do not explain deployment: the largest additions occur when low nuclear costs coincide with high gas prices. Gas prices and electricity demand also have strong associations with cost and emissions outcomes.

The results are conditional on the sampled ranges and policy assumptions; the analysis does not assign probabilities to actual policy decisions. Its published input projections cover a limited range of futures and omit some possible cost overruns and climate-related changes.
