Cooperative game theory for firm energy rights

Federal University of Itajubá · Published in Applied Energy, 2018

A reservoir can store water upstream and steady the flow through several downstream plants. Its contribution to the system may therefore be larger than the electricity it produces itself. In Brazil’s centrally coordinated hydropower system, firm energy rights help determine each plant’s share of the system’s dependable output and, in turn, how much energy its owner can sell through contracts.

The question is how to divide those rights. An allocation should recognize the value of a new plant or reservoir, while giving every group of plants at least as much as it could produce by operating on its own. We developed a method that brings those two aims together.

From each plant’s contribution to a cooperative allocation

The method starts with last addition: run the system model with all plants, then remove one plant at a time to see how much dependable output it contributes. The incremental contributions are scaled to divide the full system firm energy. This gives storage reservoirs credit for supporting downstream generation and sends a useful signal about investments in new plants. On its own, however, last addition can leave a group of plants with less than that group could produce independently.

We address that problem in three steps:

  1. Check the proposed shares. An optimization model finds the group with the largest gap between its independent firm energy and the rights allocated to its members. This avoids listing every possible group of 170 plants.

  2. Adjust the shares. A second model protects that group while keeping each plant’s percentage change from its last-addition share as small as possible.

  3. Repeat the check. Newly identified groups are protected in turn, until the largest remaining gap falls below the chosen tolerance.

The allocation rule and the meaning of the core

The core is the set of allocations for which no group of plants could obtain more firm energy by leaving the coordinated system. Let \(N\) be all plants, \(S\) a group of plants, \(v(S)\) the firm energy that group can produce on its own, and \(x_i\) the allocation to plant \(i\). An exact core allocation satisfies two conditions:

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} \sum_{i \in N} x_i &= v(N), \\ \sum_{i \in S} x_i &\ge v(S) \qquad \text{for every } S \subseteq N. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

The first distributes the full system benefit. The second ensures that no group receives less than it could produce independently. The algorithm minimizes the sum of squared percentage changes from the last-addition shares, so large plants do not dominate the adjustment simply because of their size.

In the experiments, the algorithm stops when the largest remaining coalition shortfall is below \(\varepsilon = 0.001\,v(N)\). This is a tolerance of 0.1% of total system firm energy, not 0.1% of each plant’s share. Exact core membership would require zero tolerance. See Equations (13) and (20)–(22) of the paper.

What changed in the Brazilian case

The case represents 170 Brazilian hydropower plants using 84 years of monthly inflow records. The original last-addition shares left one group short by about 2,496 MW-month. After adding 21 group constraints, the method reduced the largest remaining shortfall below the study’s tolerance of 0.1% of total system firm energy.

The next figure shows why checking only a few groups is not enough. The largest shortfall falls quickly at first, then decreases more slowly as the allocation approaches the stopping threshold.

Scaling the optimization to the national system

Finding the most under-allocated group is computationally difficult: 170 plants have an enormous number of possible groups. We reformulated the reservoir water balances so the optimization could search those groups using natural inflows and upstream storage changes. This parallel representation solves the same modeled problem much faster than the conventional series representation.

For 40 plants, the reformulation reduced reported CPU time by more than 150 times. For 170 plants, the series model ran out of memory before finding its first group constraint, while the parallel version completed the allocation. The table reports CPU seconds; the paper separately reports about 10 hours of elapsed time for the 170-plant run.

We also tested Benders decomposition with acceleration techniques. Those changes improved Benders’ performance, but the direct mixed-integer formulation was generally faster in the reported comparisons.
