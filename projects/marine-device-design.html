Joint marine energy portfolio, site, and device design

Renewable energy · Energy planning · Optimization · Published 2026

A marine energy device’s output depends on local currents, operating depth, structural limits, and the capacity of its cable to shore. This project combines engineering design and portfolio planning to choose devices and sites that make better use of a shared transmission system.

The research grew from my August 2022–August 2023 project on joint portfolio, site, and device sizing, supervised by Anderson R. de Queiroz and Chris Vermillion, and continued through subsequent NCROEP collaboration.

Connecting engineering and planning

An engineering model develops marine hydrokinetic kite designs for different flow speeds. It considers wing and fuselage geometry, buoyancy, material strength, and wing-deflection limits, producing a finite set of feasible designs for the portfolio model to compare with offshore wind turbines.

The portfolio model evaluates these candidates using site-specific wind and ocean-current data, costs, and transmission losses. It maximizes electricity delivered to shore within a levelized energy-cost limit, subject to constraints on device density, overlapping deployment areas, the collection-system radius, and export-cable capacity. Electricity above the transmission limit is curtailed, so adding devices may provide little additional energy to shore.

The North Carolina case study uses 2009–2013 resource data and compares turbine and kite combinations across transmission capacities, including 300 MW and 1,200 MW systems.

Findings

  • The preferred design varies by site. Choosing among kite designs lets the portfolio match devices to local currents.

  • Complementary devices make better use of transmission. Coordinated wind-and-kite portfolios can deliver higher average power to shore than single-technology deployments at comparable cost levels in the modeled cases.

  • Location is a compromise between resources and infrastructure. Selected developments reach toward stronger Gulf Stream currents while retaining access to shallower, less costly wind-turbine locations.

Combining the design library with a spatial portfolio model makes electricity delivered to shore the basis for comparing engineering and investment choices. Sensitivity tests vary wind, kite, and transmission costs by ±20%. The results depend on performance and cost estimates for technologies still under development; uncertainty in device design and the addition of storage remain areas for further work.
