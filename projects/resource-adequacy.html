Resource adequacy with more wind and solar

Energy planning · Renewable energy · Optimization · Resource adequacy · Research: 2024–2025

Research period: October 2024–July 2025, NREL. Supervisor: Trieu Mai. Related manuscript: Planning for Resource Adequacy Under Rising VRE Levels: A Cost-Driven Approach, with Jennie Jorgenson, Gregory Brinkman, Trieu Mai, Max Vanatta, and Rebecca Fuchs.

As wind, solar, and storage grow, the most difficult hours for a power system can shift away from its traditional demand peak. This project asks how much additional investment is needed to meet increasingly stringent resource adequacy targets, and how that cost compares with the effects of demand growth, renewable deployment, siting constraints, and interregional coordination.

A feedback loop between planning and reliability

I contributed to a workflow linking ReEDS capacity expansion with the Probabilistic Resource Adequacy Suite, PRAS. ReEDS selects investments and operating decisions; PRAS tests the resulting portfolio with chronological weather data and simulated generator outages. If a region misses its reliability target, periods of supply shortfall are passed back to ReEDS to inform the next investment decision.

The manuscript extends this process with a dynamic planning reserve margin. Each modeled investment year begins with a 4% margin. A region that fails its adequacy target receives a four-percentage-point increase alongside the newly identified stress periods. Margins and stress periods are reset at the next five-year investment step, avoiding automatic accumulation of earlier requirements.

Reliability targets and system costs

The manuscript evaluates 384 scenario combinations through 2040. These vary minimum wind and solar generation shares from 40% to 70%, electricity-demand growth, renewable siting, transmission expansion, reserve sharing, and allowable expected energy shortfalls. The reliability metric is normalized expected unserved energy: the expected shortfall divided by total electricity demand, expressed in parts per million.

Preliminary results indicate that tightening the modeled shortfall limit from 50 to 1 part per million changes 2040 annual system cost by roughly 1% or less when the other scenario conditions are held fixed. Differences in demand growth and system configuration have substantially larger cost effects. Comparing otherwise matched scenarios isolates the additional cost of the stricter adequacy target.

The findings depend on the modeled scenarios. Individual generating units and interregional trading are simplified, and the study addresses resource adequacy rather than every dimension of grid reliability. The manuscript is under DOE internal review.
