Streamflow forecasting for interconnected hydropower systems

AI · Machine learning · Hydropower · Publication: 2022

Hydropower planning depends on how much water will reach each plant over the coming days. In an interconnected basin, past flows at related plants can help forecast that water, but using every station and every past day adds redundant information. This study asks which flow records are useful for each plant and how neural-network training choices affect daily forecasts up to 14 days ahead.

The paper evaluates 55 plants in Brazil’s Paraná Basin. It forecasts natural streamflow: an estimate of incoming water that removes the effects of reservoir operations, so flows can be compared across the connected system.

Learning across the river network

For each target plant, an input-selection procedure screens up to 30 days of past flows from stations across the basin. It keeps records related to the target forecast while filtering out strongly redundant inputs. This makes the same selection process usable across a large network, rather than choosing inputs by hand for every plant.

The study uses daily records from 2000–2016, divided in time order into training, development, and test periods. It also compares ways to train the multilayer perceptron networks, including how their weights are initialized and how forecast errors are measured during training. A separate five-plant experiment asks whether past rainfall adds useful information beyond the flow records.

Forecast performance

  • Input and training choices mattered. Suitable weight initialization kept one tested model from diverging and cut training time by at least 52% in the Adam comparisons. Different training losses shifted the balance between following sudden flow peaks and fitting quieter, low-flow periods.

  • The operator comparison depended on the metric. The neural networks had lower percentage errors across the 55 plants in each year from 2014 to 2016. On Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency, which responds more strongly to flow peaks, they did better for 93% of plants in 2015 and 82% in 2016; the operator’s models did better for most plants in 2014.

  • More inputs did not automatically help. Adding historical rainfall gave no consistent improvement in the five-plant experiment, even when rainfall was accumulated over several days.

The results come from historical natural-flow records in one basin. The rainfall test uses past observations at five plants; it does not evaluate rainfall forecasts or show that rainfall could never help in another setting.
